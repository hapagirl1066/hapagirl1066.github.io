# A DIY Option for Wool Combs

![The comb structure after the epoxy has cured and hardened.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/07_Done_EpoxySetting.jpeg)

![The finished wool comb, showcasing the perfect tine spacing and final appearance.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/07_Done_TineSpacing.jpeg)

## Well, I guess I'm a spinner now.

So, I started spinning. I swore i never would, but it was inevitable that I would abjure that oath and give in. The question is just how far I would go.

The answer: I now own just-scoured fleece.

Which means I need to prep fleece.

Which means I need the *tools* to prep fleece.

So far, I've been running with DIY tools, partly because the spinning journey started as a teaching endeavor designed to show students how medieval spinning functioned as a central part of women's lives. I've enjoyed using a cheap drop spindle made out of dowels and wooden toy wheels, and plying is easier thanks to a a spindle kate made using a cheap wood-slat box and fishing spinners. Grand total: like $20.

Wool combs and carders are a different story.

## The need

As I've test-spun a few rounds of this new fleece, I realized I want a worsted prep. The cheap carders I got are fine for flick-carding, but I want the fibers aligned a little better.

There are some lovely wool combs out there that would give me the aligned fibers that I want.

What I do not want, however, is to invest in expensive combs and then end up not using them because I lose interest.

# The Solution: DIY Wool Combs

I went hunting online and found a few options, but none of them worked for the tools I had or wanted to use.
- This one uses a wood frame and two-part resin to set the tines, but I don't like working with over-the-counter two-part epoxy because it often doesn't set right when you're pouring at this depth: [https://web.archive.org/web/20251205104843/https://www.instructables.com/How-to-Easily-and-Cheaply-Make-Wool-Combs/](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205104843/https://www.instructables.com/How-to-Easily-and-Cheaply-Make-Wool-Combs/)
- This one uses plastic hair picks, which is super clever, but I wanted something a little nicer to hold and less likely to crack: [https://web.archive.org/web/20250528220514/https://www.youtube.com/watch?feature=applinks&v=xXDhEoc9AM4&pp=ygUUc2VsZWN0aW5nIHdvb2wgY29tYnM%3D](https://web.archive.org/web/20250528220514/https://www.youtube.com/watch?feature=applinks&v=xXDhEoc9AM4&pp=ygUUc2VsZWN0aW5nIHdvb2wgY29tYnM%3D)
- This one uses knitting needles, but you need a tool to cut the metal, and a little more woodworking equipment than I have: [https://looseendsfibre.co.uk/2023/10/22/diy-wool-combs/](https://looseendsfibre.co.uk/2023/10/22/diy-wool-combs/)

So I used bits and pieces of each of the DIY options above to engineer two combs and a hackle that uses a drill, the $50 mini handheld circular saw that's already in the garage, and a new $8 drill guide tool.

I'm pretty happy with how these turned out, and I think they're likely to last for a while. The first of the two combs is a little less even because I hadn't yet made a jig, but...

### Combs

Here are the supplies I used for the combs.

## Sketching the Design and Tine Spacing

I played with the length of the wool comb and the right spacing to accommodate the 8mm nailheads but keep the nail shafts close enough together to be effective for combing. 9.5mm or 10mm from center to center worked best.

![Initial sketch drawing showing the pattern for tine spacing.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/02_Drafting_SpacingSketch.jpeg)

![Physical test piece demonstrating the calculated tine spacing.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/02_Drafting_SpacingTestIMG_3621.jpeg)

## Cutting the Paddles

Draw a line 2 1/2" from the start of the handle curve and use a circular or handsaw to cut across the paddle on that line to form your comb. If you're going to make a hackle, cut one of the remaining rectangles in half lengthwise.

Sand down the sharp edges of the new wool combs before proceeding.

![The large wooden paddles with all the required tine markings drawn onto the surface.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/03_Marking_Paddles.jpeg)

## Tine-Spacing Guide

You should not mark your paddles manually. ***Instead, [download this printable tine-spacing guide]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/WoolCombSpacingGuide.pdf).*** Print one and tape it to the paddle for spacing the tines; fold it around the straight edge of the hackle piece. The pink and blue lines are offset so that you can drill a one-row comb using only the pink grid or a two-row comb using first the pink grid and then the blue grid.

I folded the tine-spacing guide on two sides, pinched it around the paddle and then taped the handle to the paper guide so that I could flip the whole thing over.

![The jig placed on the workspace, aligning with the marked paddles.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/05_Prepping_JigPlacement.jpeg)

![The jig and paddles secured with tape to prevent shifting.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/05_Prepping_JigTaping.jpeg)

## Drilling and Assembly

With the tine-spacing guide secured, I used the pink and blue grid to align the drill guide. If the drill guide is unstable, slip extra pieces of the paddle under the tine-spacing guide to make it easier to align.

![The fully supported and prepared assembly, ready for drilling.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/05_Prepping_JigTapingDrillSupport.jpeg)

Because the drill guide didn't have the right size to fit the 3.3mm nails, I used a 1/8" drill bit first to create a guide hole for each tine hole. With the drill guide, it's easiest to work from the hole furthest away to the hole closest to you. Otherwise, you'll end up drilling through the guide lines on the printed drill guide and its harder to line up the holes correctly.

To enlarge the holes while keeping them straight up and down, I used a 5/32" drill bit, pressing very gently at first to get the larger drill bit to engage and follow the pre-drilled 1/8 guide hole.

![Aligning the drill guide over the pre-drilled holes.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/06_Assembly_DrillGuideAlignment.jpeg)

Sand everything on both sides, and then insert the nails from the bottom of the paddle, away from the side where you initially drilled.

Pop the nails out partway and apply mixed JBWeld to the nail shaft just under the nail head.

![The initial application of epoxy resin to the assembly.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/06_Assembly_Epoxy.jpeg)

![The assembly completely coated with epoxy resin.]({{site.urlimg}}woolcombs/06_Assembly_EpoxyApplied.jpeg)

## Curing and Finishing

Prop the finished combs on the edge of a surface where they won't get disturbed, and use a counterweight on the paddles to hold them down.

Wait for the epoxy to cure, and voila! Wool Combs!

## Hackle

After you cut the paddle handle off to use for the combs, you'll have a 4" wide, 8" long rectangle.

Cut this in half longways and sand down the edge.

Mark 3/4" to 1" on either end.

Fold a printed drill guide around the sharp edge and anchor it in place. Do not let this slip.

Drill guide holes for tines along the sharp edge of the new hackle, leaving the two ends you just marked undrilled.

Install the tines as you did with the combs and let the epoxy cure.

Turn the hackle so you can see the side of the hackle, with the tines closest to you.

Mark two holes 3/4" from both ends. Drill using a drill bit large enough to fit an M4 L-bolt.

Coat the smooth ends of 2 M4 L-bolts with epoxy and insert them into these new holes. Cure the epoxy so that the M4 bolts are square.

Cut 2 pieces of 2"x4" wood from the second spare paddle leftover. Drill 2 holes 1/2 from the front of these 2 blocks.

Stick adhesive rubber to the back half of these spare blocks, just behind the drilled holes.

Slip these new blocks over the threaded part of the M4 bolts and loosely screw on the butterfly bolts.

Slip the hackle onto a table and tighten the butterfly bolts to anchor the hackle in place.
